# Diagram 8 cannot provide a nonpositive singular place for positive mass ratios other than one quarter

**Chatgpt · Research note · 27 November 2026**

Consider six positive masses proportional to $(1,1,1,1,1,t)$. For a singular place let $L$ be the sum of the fifteen normalized orders of the squared mutual distances. For every positive $t$ other than $1/4$, diagram 8 cannot occur at a place with $L\le 0$. This includes all labelled placements of the distinguished body. The claim concerns the obstruction required by Tomar’s Corollary 2.7. Places with $L>0$ are outside its scope, and existence at $t=1/4$ remains undecided. The argument combines two criteria in Tomar’s §9 with an exhaustive check of the cells already enumerated in the same preprint. [Tomar, Propositions 3.1, 4.5 and §9.3](https://hexagonmath.org/pdf/2611.00377v1)

## Why the two tests suffice

Chang–Chen diagram 8 is class 29 in Tomar’s data. There are 1,866 listed cells. Proposition 4.5 guarantees that all cells of this class with $L\le 0$ are listed. For a triple $T$ to be a triangle in Tomar’s sense, its three labels must lie below distinct children of one node in each of the two coordinate trees. The two nodes can be different. A body is balanced in one coordinate if its lowest-order equation contains exactly the two forces from the other members of $T$.

- **1,842 cells pass the balanced-triangle test.** At least two bodies are balanced in one coordinate and at least one in the other. Corollary 9.10 requires one of the three inverse square roots of the masses to equal the sum of the other two. An all-unit triple cannot satisfy this. For masses $(1,1,t)$, positivity leaves only $1/\sqrt t=2$. Hence any such cell requires $t=1/4$.
- **The other 24 pass an equal-force test.** In each cell there is a triangle $T=\{a,b,s\}$ and a choice of coordinate $c$ for which $a$ and $b$ are both balanced. The mass-weighted sum of the triangle’s internal forces is zero, so the force on $s$ also vanishes at this order in $c$. In the other coordinate the lowest-order equations for $a,b$ consist of their positions and the two forces from $T$. Their positions have the same nonzero leading coefficient. Indeed, the least common ancestor of $a,b$ in this coordinate tree lies strictly below their meeting with the origin. The leading terms of the differences $a-O$ and $b-O$ thus use the same two distinct child coordinates, in the same order (Tomar’s Lemma 4.2). Each of the force sums therefore equals this common position coefficient. Theorem 9.9(ii) now requires $m_s^2(m_a+m_b)^4=m_a^3m_b^3$. All-unit masses fail this equation. A variable $m_s=t$ gives $16t^2=1$, hence $t=1/4$. A variable $m_a$ or $m_b$ would require $(1+t)^4=t^3$, impossible for $t>0$.

Cell 1617 illustrates why a zero in one certificate need not leave a case open. In ordinary one-based body labels it contains two triangles satisfying Corollary 9.10, namely $\{2,5,6\}$ and $\{3,4,6\}$. In each of the two coordinates bodies 2 and 5 see only their partner and body 6 at the lowest order, and the same is true of 3 and 4 in the other triangle. For this cell both necessary mass relations can hold only if the variable body is 6 and $t=1/4$. The cell number and class number are the zero-based catalogue identifiers.
